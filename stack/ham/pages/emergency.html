Emergency nets and messages

Summarised from the ARRL NTS Methods and Practices Guidelines (Public Service Communications Manual, Appendix B: ch. 1 "The ARRL Message Format" and ch. 4 "Net Operations") and from FCC Part 97. The FCC rule text is at the bottom of this page. For local plans, follow your ARES Emergency Coordinator.

In a real emergency

  • When normal communications are down, an amateur may use any means of radio communication at its disposal for the immediate safety of life and protection of property (§ 97.403). A station in distress may use any means to get attention and help, and others may use any means to help it (§ 97.405).
  • Listen first. Use the local ARES/RACES net if one is running; otherwise start on a known repeater or the national simplex frequencies (146.520, 446.000 MHz).
  • To interrupt a conversation with emergency traffic, say "Break" or "Emergency" plus your call sign in a pause, then wait to be acknowledged. Everyone else stands by. (Common practice; not an FCC rule.)
  • Still identify with your call sign at the end of each exchange and at least every 10 minutes (§ 97.119). Tactical calls ("Shelter 2", "Net Control") are fine in addition, but not instead.

How a directed net works

In a directed net, all traffic goes through the Net Control Station (NCS); you speak only when NCS calls you or acknowledges you. An open (free) net lets stations call each other directly. Emergency nets are usually directed.

  1. Net opens. NCS calls the net, identifies, says it is a directed net, reads any preamble, and first asks "stations with emergency traffic?" — that traffic is handled immediately.
  2. Checking in. When NCS asks for check-ins, give your call sign once, clearly (phonetics if conditions are poor), plus anything NCS asks for: name, location, and whether you have traffic, e.g. "N0CALL, Pat, Springfield, no traffic" or "N0CALL, one priority for Red Cross".
  3. Wait to be recognised. Don't call other stations or comment until NCS asks. If you need NCS, give just your call sign in a gap and wait.
  4. Passing traffic. NCS pairs a sender and receiver and may send them off frequency ("N0CALL and W2ABC, up 15"). Pass the message, then return and report back to NCS with your suffix.
  5. Leaving. Ask NCS to be excused; sign with your full call sign when you go. NCS closes the net with its full call sign.

Keep transmissions short; say "over" only where needed (e.g. on repeaters with delay); and let the repeater key up before you speak.

Message precedence

PrecedenceCWUse
EMERGENCYspelled outLife-and-death urgency, sent by radio because normal channels are unavailable; includes official relief requests for vital supplies. Rare. When in doubt, don't use it.
PRIORITYPImportant messages with a time limit; official messages not covered by Emergency; emergency-related press; notice of death or injury in a disaster area.
WELFAREWHealth-and-welfare inquiries about someone in a disaster area, or "all is well" from there. Handled after Emergency and Priority.
ROUTINEREverything else. In a disaster, last or not at all.

Radiogram format (ARRL message)

A formal message has four parts:

  1. Preamble: number, precedence, handling instructions (optional), station of origin, check (word count of the text), place of origin, time filed (optional), date.
  2. Address: name, street, city/state/ZIP, phone.
  3. Text: short; "X" for a full stop; counted in words for the check.
  4. Signature: who the message is from.

Read it slowly, by parts, and say "break" between the parts. The receiver repeats anything uncertain, and spells unusual words phonetically. Example preamble on voice: "Number two zero seven, Routine, N0CALL, check ten, Springfield IL, November three".

Quick list

  • National simplex: 146.520 MHz (2 m), 446.000 MHz (70 cm).
  • Weather and emergency alerts: NOAA Weather Radio, see nearest transmitters.
  • Non-ham neighbours: FRS/GMRS channels and CB channel 9 (emergencies) on the channels page; boats on marine channel 16 (marine).
